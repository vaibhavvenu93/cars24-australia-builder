_Last updated: August 2026_

# 1. Executive Summary

Australia's used-car market is large, mature, fragmented and becoming increasingly competitive.

The market appears to have moved through three distinct phases:

### 2020–2022
Supply-constrained market.

- new vehicle shortages
- limited used inventory
- inflated used-car prices
- abnormal vehicle appreciation
- unusually favourable conditions for inventory holders

### 2023–2025
Normalisation.

- new-car supply recovered
- trade-ins and fleet turnover increased
- dealer used inventory rebuilt
- used prices declined from COVID-era peaks
- buyer choice improved
- dealer participation increased

### 2026
Supply and competitive intensity appear to be increasing faster than demand.

The emerging operating challenge is therefore less:

> "Can we find cars?"

and increasingly:

> "Can we buy the right cars, at the right price, recondition them efficiently, merchandise them correctly and sell them quickly enough to preserve margin and working capital?"

This distinction matters enormously for a full-stack inventory-owning business such as CARS24.

# 2. Market Size

Australia recorded approximately:

**2.32 million used vehicle sales in 2025.**

This was only around 0.37% below 2024, suggesting the market was broadly stable following several years of post-COVID disruption.

Dealer transactions represented:

**48.6% of used-car sales in 2025**

versus approximately:

**39.1% in 2024.**

This is a significant structural shift.

Potential interpretation:

Consumers may increasingly value the trust, financing, warranty, convenience and transaction support offered through dealer channels.

For a business such as CARS24, this supports the broad thesis that a professionally managed full-stack used-car proposition has room to win share from private transactions.

However, dealer participation also creates greater competitive intensity.

---

# 3. 2026 Has Become More Difficult

The first half of 2026 shows a materially different environment.

Approximately:

**1.30 million used vehicles**

were sold nationally during H1 2026.

That represented:

**-6.6% YoY**

versus H1 2025.

The deterioration accelerated during the period.

By June 2026:

**used-car sales were down 16.2% YoY.**

At the same time:

- inventory increased
- buyer choice increased
- discounting increased
- vehicles took longer to sell

This combination matters.

It suggests that the market is moving towards greater buyer power.

---

# 4. Supply Is Growing Against Softer Demand

By July 2026:

**437,855 vehicles** were listed nationally.

Monthly supply increased:

**13%**

while monthly sales increased:

**11.8%.**

Approximately:

**240,311 vehicles**

were sold during July.

The market can therefore simultaneously experience:

- high transaction activity
- high inventory
- slower turnover

July average selling time reached:

**52.1 days.**

This was the highest point of 2026 at the time.

This creates an important distinction:

> Market activity ≠ inventory efficiency.

For an inventory-owning platform, the relevant question is not simply how many vehicles sell.

It is:

**How long does each dollar of inventory capital remain deployed before it returns as cash and gross profit?**

---

# 5. Dealer Inventory Velocity Looks Particularly Important

AADA/AutoGrab data for July 2026 indicates that dealers were taking approximately:

**22 days longer**

than private sellers to sell equivalent vehicles aged 1–5 years.

Dealer share during July was:

**42.1%.**

Dealer volumes grew 9.1%.

Private seller volume grew:

**26.2%.**

This does not necessarily mean dealer propositions are weakening.

But it does suggest:

**inventory velocity deserves serious investigation.**

Possible reasons include:

- dealer pricing
- higher transaction price
- larger inventory breadth
- mix differences
- financing processes
- buyer behaviour
- geography
- merchandising
- competition
- inventory selection

The evidence specifically warns against assuming slower turnover is purely a pricing problem.

AADA notes that dealers achieved similar retained values while discounting less frequently and less deeply than private sellers.

Therefore:

> velocity may be influenced by multiple operational and market variables simultaneously.

---

# 6. Discounting Is Increasing

By May 2026:

more than **50% of used vehicles sold** had undergone an asking-price reduction.

Average discounts reached:

**3.4%.**

By June:

average discounts widened further to:

**3.7%.**

This implies greater competitive pricing pressure.

For an inventory-owning business, the sequence becomes important:

Acquisition price
→ refurbishment cost
→ initial list price
→ inventory age
→ repricing
→ final sale price
→ contribution margin

A mistake at acquisition can therefore become visible weeks later as:

- ageing inventory
- deeper discounting
- lower GPU
- capital lockup

This supports treating pricing as a lifecycle system rather than a single transaction.

# 7. Used-Car Prices Have Normalised From COVID Peaks

Cox Automotive Australia data shows that used-car prices declined significantly from their 2022 peaks.

For example, its dealer used vehicle price index:

- peaked around 148.1 in August 2022
- fell to approximately 131 by mid-2024

Wholesale values also declined substantially from pandemic highs.

This matters for inventory-owning companies.

During rapidly rising markets:

inventory can appreciate.

During declining or competitive markets:

**inventory becomes a depreciating asset.**

Therefore every unnecessary inventory day potentially creates:

- depreciation exposure
- working-capital cost
- markdown risk
- registration/storage cost
- servicing requirements
- opportunity cost

---

# 8. Market Days Supply Is a Core Metric

Cox Automotive defines Market Days Supply as:

Available inventory relative to recent sales velocity.

Historically, Cox has used approximately:

**70 days**

as a benchmark.

Below this level generally indicates stronger demand relative to supply.

Above it suggests oversupply.

Even where CARS24 does not use precisely the same metric internally, the underlying concept is important.

A Builder should understand:

### Inventory Days

How long does a vehicle remain available?

### Acquisition-to-Listing

How much time passes before inventory can generate demand?

### Listing-to-Sale

How quickly does demand convert?

### Total Capital Cycle

How long from acquisition payment until sale proceeds return?

These should be separated.

---

# 9. The Used-Car Business Is Really a Working-Capital Business

A simplified vehicle-level model:

Purchase price

+ acquisition cost

+ inspection

+ transport

+ refurbishment

+ storage

+ registration

+ marketing

+ financing cost

= invested capital

The asset then sits until sold.

Therefore:

**Vehicle Gross Profit alone is an incomplete measure.**

Two vehicles earning A$3,000 GPU can have very different economics.

Example:

Vehicle A

A$3,000 GPU  
20 inventory days

Vehicle B

A$3,000 GPU  
90 inventory days

Vehicle A can recycle capital more than four times faster.

This makes return-on-inventory-capital potentially more valuable than simply gross profit per unit.

Potential metric:

**Gross Profit × Inventory Turns**

or

**Contribution Margin / Capital Days**

This should be explored later.

# 10. Carma Provides a Useful Public Benchmark

CARS24 does not publicly expose detailed Australian unit economics.

Carma does.

Because Carma runs a structurally similar model, its filings provide useful directional evidence about the economics of full-stack digital used-car retail.

Carma:

- acquires vehicles
- inspects them
- reconditions them
- owns inventory
- sells online
- offers financing
- delivers vehicles
- offers returns
- operates wholesale channels

Its prospectus explicitly identifies the following unit-economic levers:

### Buying margin

Acquire vehicles below expected retail value.

### Reconditioning cost

Reduce refurbishment expense through operational scale.

### Inventory turnover

Sell vehicles faster.

### Customer acquisition cost

Increase direct acquisition and brand traffic.

### Value-added products

Finance, warranty and other ancillary revenue.

### Fixed-cost leverage

Spread operating cost across more vehicles.

This gives us a useful external model for CARS24.

# 14. Vehicle Sourcing Is Not Just About Volume

A naïve growth model says:

More cars acquired → more inventory → more sales.

But a full-stack retailer can destroy economics by acquiring the wrong vehicles.

The true objective should be closer to:

**Acquire inventory with the highest probability-adjusted return on capital.**

That requires understanding:

- expected demand
- expected selling price
- refurbishment requirements
- local demand density
- financing demand
- age
- mileage
- model
- segment
- depreciation
- market supply
- acquisition source

Therefore supply acquisition itself may become a predictive intelligence problem.

# 20. Finance Is Both a Conversion Lever and Risk Area

Used vehicles represent a substantial portion of Australian automotive finance.

Finance can improve:

- affordability
- conversion
- ASP
- ancillary margin

However, regulatory scrutiny is increasing.

ASIC's recent review of motor vehicle finance highlighted:

- early defaults
- high establishment costs in some cases
- consumer vulnerability
- lender/distributor oversight

For CARS24, finance optimisation therefore cannot simply mean:

> maximise finance penetration.

**conversion × customer affordability × compliance × credit quality × trust.**

# 21. Digital Automotive Retail Still Has Headroom

Australia has high general e-commerce adoption.

However, true end-to-end online vehicle purchasing remains relatively underpenetrated compared with mature online retail categories.

Research included in Carma's prospectus found:

approximately **25% of buyers globally intended to purchase vehicles online in 2024**, up from 18% in 2021.

Yet actual end-to-end digital car purchasing in Australia remains relatively limited.

Therefore CARS24 and Carma are not merely competing with each other.

Dealership-first

to:

Digital-first.

This means **trust becomes fundamental infrastructure.**
